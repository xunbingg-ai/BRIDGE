# 4. Chief Complaint
Recurrent chest pain on and off for 4 weeks.

# 5. History of Present Illness
Ms. Chen is a 32-year-old previously healthy woman who presents with a 4-week history of intermittent sharp pain over the bilateral anterior chest. Each episode lasts for about 30 minutes and occurs on and off, particularly when she is stressed at work. The pain does not radiate to her arm, jaw, back, or epigastrium. She has tried ibuprofen and antacid, but neither has provided clear relief. She reports no heartburn, fever, cough, exertional dyspnoea, syncope, diaphoresis, or palpitations. She goes to the gym regularly and does not experience chest pain during exercise.

She has no known previous cardiac disease and takes no regular medications. She is particularly worried that the pain may be related to a heart problem because her father recently had a heart attack, and she is requesting a full cardiac check-up.

# 17. Working Diagnosis

Most likely diagnosis: Stress/anxiety-related chest pain
Rationale: Pain occurs during work stress, Non-exertional pattern, No pain during exercise, Normal vital signs and physical examination, Normal ECG, No symptoms suggesting respiratory infection or reflux, Significant health anxiety related to father’s recent heart attack.

# 18. Differential Diagnosis

1) Angina/Coronary heart disease: Less likely but important to consider because she smokes, has mildly elevated LDL-C and family history of cardiovascular disease. And She is concerned about heart attack.
2) Musculoskeletal chest pain/costochondritis: Possible because sharp anterior chest pain can be musculoskeletal, but it's less likely because there's no clear relation to movement or exercise, no relief with ibuprofen and no chest wall tenderness on exam.
3) Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease/oesophageal spasm: lest likely because there's no heartburn, no relief with antacid and pain is associated with stress rather than meals or lying down.
4) Respiratory causes like pneumonia, pleuritis, pneumothorax: Less likely because there's no fever/cough/dyspnoea, and normal VS & respiratory exam.
5) Panic attack/anxiety disorder: Possible because symptoms are stress-related and she has health anxiety. Needs further assessment (eg. Palpitations, sweating, trembling, breathlessness, fear of dying, avoidance behaviour, insomnia, low mood, functional impairment and duration of anxiety symptoms).

# 19. Other Problem List

1) Current smoker — 1 pack per day.
2) Work-related chronic stress.
3) Health anxiety regarding myocardial infarction.
4) Frequent fast-food intake / unhealthy diet.
5) Mildly elevated LDL-C.
6) Borderline total cholesterol and HDL-C.
7) Family history of hypertension and MI in father.
8) Separated from husband 2 years ago — possible psychosocial stressor.

# 20. Management

1) Pharmacological: 
- Nil for chest pain or lipid mangement. 
- If she is motivated to quit or reduce smoking, offer first-line nicotine replacement therapy, with counselling.
Options include: 
a.Nicotine gum (eg. Nicotine gum 2 mg or 4 mg as needed for cravings, 4 mg may be considered if high dependence or smoking soon after waking, Chew-and-park technique should be explained.) Common adverse effects include Mouth irritation, Hiccups, Nausea, Jaw discomfort.
b.Nicotine patch (eg. Nicotine transdermal patch 21 mg once daily for 4–6 weeks if smoking ≥10 cigarettes/day, then step down to 14 mg daily for 2–4 weeks, then 7 mg daily for 2–4 weeks.) Precautions for Skin irritation, Insomnia or vivid dreams — remove patch at night if troublesome, Avoid excessive nicotine exposure, Continue behavioural support.
c.Combination therapy: Nicotine patch plus gum/lozenge for breakthrough cravings can be considered, especially for heavy smokers.
d.Other options include Varenicline or Bupropion, requiring assessment of contraindications, mental health history, pregnancy status, and drug interactions.

2) Non-Pharmacological Management
a. Reassurance and explanation - pain pattern is not typical for angina, the most likely trigger appears to be stress rather than a heart attack, immediate risk appears low, but smoking, stress, and LDL cholesterol can increase long-term cardiovascular risk.
b. Stress management: Mindfulness or breathing exercises, Brief relaxation exercises during work breaks, Journaling, Talking to trusted friends or family, Regular exercise, Sleep hygiene. Consider counselling or psychological support if anxiety persists. Screen for generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, depression, and self-harm if clinically indicated.
c. Smoking cessation counselling
d. Diet modification: Advise - Reduce saturated fats, trans fats, refined sugars, and excessive salt. Increase vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, fish, nuts, and lean proteins. Choose unsaturated fats, e.g., olive oil, nuts, avocado. Avoid sugary drinks. 
e. Physical activity: Encourage continuation of regular exercise. 

3) Follow-up Plan: 
a. Short-term: Follow up in 2–4 weeks to review Chest pain frequency and triggers, Anxiety/stress symptoms, Smoking cessation readiness and NRT use if started, Any new red flag symptoms.
b. Lipid follow-up: Repeat lipid panel in 3 months after dietary and lifestyle changes. Reassess ASCVD risk and consider whether further intervention c. Safety-netting: Seek medical attention if develops exertional chest pressure, chest pain radiating to arm/jaw, Syncope, Severe breathlessness, Persistent chest pain >15–20 minutes, Sweating, nausea, or collapse.

# 21. Outcome
At 1-month telephone follow-up, the patient reported reduced work-related stress and less frequent chest pain episodes. She had stopped smoking for 1 week and did not require nicotine replacement therapy at this stage.

# 22. Structured Reasoning Q&As

## Diagnostic Reasoning 

1. Based on the history, please state the most likely diagnosis and list at least two additional differential diagnoses. Briefly explain your reasoning.

1a. Recommended Answer: 
The most likely diagnosis is **stress/anxiety-related chest pain**.
Supporting features:
- Chest pain occurs during stressful work situations.
- Pain is intermittent and sharp.
- No exertional chest pain despite regular gym exercise.
- No fever, cough, or heartburn.
- No relief with ibuprofen or antacid.
- Strong concern about heart attack after her father’s MI, suggesting health anxiety.

Differential diagnoses include:
1. **Angina / coronary heart disease** — important to consider due to smoking, mildly elevated LDL, and family history, but less likely because symptoms are non-exertional and ECG is normal.
2. **Musculoskeletal chest pain / costochondritis** — sharp anterior chest pain may be musculoskeletal, but exam is negative and no response to ibuprofen.
3. **GERD / oesophageal spasm** — can cause chest discomfort, but no heartburn and no response to antacid.
4. **Respiratory causes such as pneumonia or pleuritis** — less likely due to no fever, cough, dyspnoea, or abnormal lung findings.
5. **Panic attack / anxiety disorder** — possible and should be further assessed.


2. Based on the history, what physical examination would you perform, and what signs would you assess for?

2a. Recommended Answer
Perform:
1)**Vital signs**
   - Temperature: fever suggesting infection.
   - BP: hypertension as ASCVD risk factor.
   - Pulse: tachycardia or arrhythmia.
   - RR and SpO₂: respiratory compromise.
   - Height, weight, BMI: obesity and cardiovascular risk.
2)**General examination**
   - Distress, diaphoresis, pallor, anxiety, respiratory effort.
3)**Chest wall and skin**
   - Rash or vesicles suggesting herpes zoster.
   - Bruising or trauma.
   - Local tenderness suggesting musculoskeletal pain or costochondritis.
4)**Respiratory examination**
   - Reduced breath sounds, crackles, wheeze, pleural rub.
   - Signs suggesting pneumonia, pneumothorax, asthma, or pleuritis.
5)**Cardiovascular examination**
   - Heart sounds, murmurs, extra heart sounds, rhythm.
   - Pericardial rub.
   - JVP, peripheral pulses.
   - Signs of heart failure.
6)**Extremities**
   - Oedema suggesting cardiac disease.
   - Calf swelling or tenderness if PE is suspected.

3. Based on the history and physical exam findings, please propose appropriate investigations and briefly explain your reasoning.

3a. Recommended Answer
Because she is low risk for acute coronary syndrome but concerned about heart disease, investigations should be guided by shared decision-making.
Appropriate investigations include:
1)**12-lead ECG**
   - Baseline assessment.
   - Helps exclude obvious arrhythmia or acute ischaemic changes.
   - Provides reassurance.
2)**Random glucose / fasting glucose / HbA1c**
   - Screens for diabetes as a cardiovascular risk factor.
3)**Lipid panel**
   - Assesses ASCVD risk.
   - Especially relevant because she smokes and frequently eats fast food.
4)Not routinely indicated at this stage:
- Troponin, unless symptoms suggest acute coronary syndrome.
- Chest X-ray, unless respiratory symptoms or abnormal lung examination are present.
- Stress test or coronary CT, unless exertional symptoms or higher cardiac risk features develop.

4. Please interpret the available investigation results, give your current working diagnosis and assessment, and outline the patient’s problem list.

4a. Recommended Answer
1)Investigation Interpretation
- ECG: normal. No acute ischaemic changes or arrhythmia.
- Random glucose: 5.1 mmol/L, normal.
- Lipid panel:
  - TC 5.2 mmol/L: borderline high / upper limit.
  - TG 1.5 mmol/L: normal.
  - LDL-C 3.8 mmol/L: mildly elevated.
  - HDL-C 1.3 mmol/L: borderline lower acceptable limit for a woman.
2)Working Diagnosis: Stress/anxiety-related chest pain. Current chest pain is unlikely to be due to acute coronary syndrome or angina because symptoms are non-exertional, stress-associated, and investigations/examination are reassuring. However, she has modifiable long-term cardiovascular risk factors, especially smoking, unhealthy diet, chronic stress, and mildly elevated LDL-C.

## Management Reasoning

5. What pharmacological treatment would you initiate for this patient, and why?

5a. Recommended Answer
No cardiac medication is needed at this stage because the presentation is low risk and ECG is normal.
For smoking cessation, pharmacological treatment can be offered if she is ready:
- First-line: **Nicotine replacement therapy**, such as nicotine patch or nicotine gum.
- For someone smoking 1 pack/day, a nicotine patch can be considered, with gum or lozenge for breakthrough cravings.
Statin is not started immediately because she is young with low short-term ASCVD risk and only mildly elevated LDL-C. Lifestyle modification and repeat lipid testing in 3 months are appropriate.

6. What non-pharmacological management would you recommend for this patient, and why?

6a. Recommended Answer
1)Reassurance and explanation: Explain that her pain pattern and normal tests suggest low likelihood of heart attack. Validate her fear because of her father’s MI.
2)Introduce stress management techniques like Mindfulness, Breathing exercises, Journaling, Talking with friends/family, Counselling if persistent anxiety.
3)Smoking cessation counselling: Discuss triggers and readiness, Create a quit plan, Offer behavioural support and NRT.
4）**Dietary modification**：Reduce fast food, saturated fat, trans fat, refined sugars, and salt. Increase vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats.

7. Please outline your follow-up plan for this patient.

7a. Recommended Answer
a. Short-term: Follow up in 2–4 weeks to review Chest pain frequency and triggers, Anxiety/stress symptoms, Smoking cessation readiness and NRT use if started, Any new red flag symptoms.
b. Lipid follow-up: Repeat lipid panel in 3 months after dietary and lifestyle changes. Reassess ASCVD risk and consider whether further intervention c. Safety-netting: Seek medical attention if develops exertional chest pressure, chest pain radiating to arm/jaw, Syncope, Severe breathlessness, Persistent chest pain >15–20 minutes, Sweating, nausea, or collapse.
